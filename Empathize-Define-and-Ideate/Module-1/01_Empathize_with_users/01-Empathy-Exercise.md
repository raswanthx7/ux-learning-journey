# 1. Empathy in UX Design

Empathy is a pillar of good UX design because it helps designers connect with users and understand their needs and problems.

Designers can begin empathizing before formal user research to:

- Understand users' perspectives
- Anticipate possible experiences
- Identify possible needs and pain points
- Refine research plans
- Improve interview questions
- Reduce assumptions and bias

---

# 2. What Is an Empathy Exercise?

An **empathy exercise** is when designers use imagination, perspective-taking skills, and existing information about users to anticipate how users might experience a product or situation.

The purpose is **not to predict exact user answers**.

It is to:

- Think about possible experiences
- Identify assumptions
- Consider possible needs and concerns
- Improve interview questions
- Prepare for real user interviews

> **Important:** Empathy exercise ≠ real user research.

An empathy exercise produces **hypotheses**; real interviews provide **evidence**.

---

# 3. User Profiles vs. User Personas

## User Profile

A **user profile** is a short, fictional biography used for an informal empathy exercise.

## User Persona

A **user persona** has a more formal role in the UX design process.

Personas are created after interviewing many users and identifying common characteristics.

### Simple Difference

**User Profile → Fictional → Used for empathy exercises**

**User Persona → Based on real research → Used throughout the design process**

# 4. Example: Zia's Pizza App

The designer is preparing to interview users for the **Zia's Pizza app** and has already established interview goals and questions.

---

# 5. Interview Goals

### Goal 1

Understand common challenges people face when:

- Managing a busy schedule
- Planning meals

### Goal 2

Identify frustrations people experience during:

- The restaurant ordering process

### Why Define Research Goals?

Research goals explain:

> **What do we want to learn from the users?**

They help keep interview questions focused on the information the designer actually needs.

---

# 6. Original Interview Questions

1. What is your current schedule like? How do you balance your responsibilities with meal planning?
2. How often do you order meals from a restaurant? When you do, what is your motivation?
3. What challenges do you face in the ordering process? How do these questions make you feel?
4. How do you think these challenges could be resolved?

# 7. Fictional User Profile: Reena

**Age:** 37  
**Hometown:** Lakewood, Colorado  
**Family:** Lives mostly alone and has a teenage daughter  
**Occupation:** Paramedic

Reena is a paramedic who lives in Lakewood, Colorado. She lives alone but shares custody of her daughter with her ex-partner.

She works both day and night shifts, and her schedule changes from week to week.

She is a rock climber who goes to the climbing gym a few mornings a week. She also drives her daughter to soccer practice on nights she is not working.

Reena has dyslexia, which makes it difficult to read text quickly and process information in fast-paced situations.

She is:

- Health-conscious
- Budget-conscious

---

# 8. Pre-Interview Empathy Exercise

Before interviewing people like Reena, the designer performs a **pre-interview empathy exercise**.

The designer imagines Reena as a real person and thinks about:

- How Reena might answer the interview questions
- What her experiences might be
- What needs she might have
- What challenges or concerns she might experience
- How those needs might potentially be addressed

### Important

The designer is **not claiming that Reena actually thinks or behaves this way**.

These are hypotheses used to prepare for the interview.

---

# 9. Perspective Taking

**Perspective taking** means trying to look at a situation from another person's point of view.

Instead of asking:

> "How would I use this pizza app?"

The designer thinks:

> "Considering Reena's schedule, responsibilities, dyslexia, health concerns, and budget, what might this experience be like?"

This helps designers consider experiences they might otherwise overlook.

---

# 10. Identifying Possible Needs, Challenges, and Pain Points

Possible hypotheses about Reena could include:

- Difficulty browsing many restaurants
- Difficulty reading large amounts of information
- Difficulty processing information quickly
- Difficulty comparing meals
- Ordering when tired after a long shift
- Difficulty finding frequently ordered restaurants
- Staying within her budget
- Finding healthy meal options

These are **possible hypotheses, not confirmed research findings**.

---

# 11. Identifying Assumptions

Identifying assumptions means finding things the designer believes might be true but has not yet validated with evidence.

### Example

> "Reena will have difficulty reading information on a small phone screen."

This may be reasonable based on the profile, but it is still an assumption until real research confirms it.

Another assumption:

> "Busy people need a Favorites feature."

Maybe they do. Maybe they don't.

The interview should help discover what is actually true.

### Key Principle

> **Assumption = Something you believe might be true but have not validated with evidence yet.**

---

# 12. Leading Questions

The question:

> "What challenges do you face in the ordering process?"

can be problematic because the word **"challenges"** assumes that the participant has challenges.

The participant may actually think:

> "I don't really have any problems with ordering food."

But the question has already suggested that there should be a problem.

Therefore, it can influence the participant's answer.

---

# 13. Refined Interview Question

A more neutral version is:

> **"What is the ordering process like for you? Tell me about the experience you have with ordering and how it makes you feel."**

This allows participants to discuss:

- Positive experiences
- Negative experiences
- Problems
- Frustrations
- Easy parts
- Difficult parts
- Anything else they consider important

---

# 14. Follow-Up Questions

The designer does not completely remove the word **"challenges."**

Instead, it can be used as a follow-up if the participant introduces a challenge themselves.

### Example

**User:**

> "Sometimes I have trouble finding what I want."

**Designer:**

> "Tell me more about that challenge."

### Interview Flow

**Broad question → User responds → Follow the user's answer → Ask a relevant follow-up**

---

# 15. Don't Force the Problem

A UX researcher should not try to make every user say they have a problem.

Some users may say:

> "Everything works fine for me."

That is valuable research data too.

**Positive feedback is data.**

**Negative feedback is data.**

**No problem is also data.**

The goal is to discover what is actually true.

---

# 16. Empathy Exercises and Interview Questions

Empathy exercises help designers review their research plans.

Ask yourself:

- Am I making assumptions?
- Is this question leading?
- Am I suggesting a problem?
- Am I suggesting a solution?
- Could the user interpret this question differently?
- Can I make the question more open-ended?
- Am I allowing the user to describe their own experience?

# 18. Analyze the Data

After empathy exercises or real interviews:

### Step 1 — Review What You Wrote

Look at:

- Experiences
- Needs
- Concerns
- Problems
- Behaviors
- Motivations

### Step 2 — Compare Users

Look for things that appear across multiple users.

| User | Possible Need |
|---|---|
| Reena | Quickly find frequently ordered restaurants |
| User B | Quickly reorder meals |
| User C | Avoid spending too much time browsing |
| User D | Easily find preferred restaurants |

Possible common pattern:

> **Users want to quickly access restaurants or meals they already like.**

---

# 19. Identify Commonalities and Patterns

A UX designer should look for **patterns across users**.

One person's experience does not automatically represent everyone.

But if several users independently mention similar things, the pattern becomes more meaningful.

### Example

**Reena:** Frequently orders from the same restaurants.

**User B:** Doesn't want to browse restaurants every time.

**User C:** Wants quick access to previous orders.

### Possible Commonality

> **Users value faster access to familiar choices.**

---

# 20. Envision Common Solutions

Once common needs or problems are identified, designers can think about possible features.

### Example

**User Need:**

Busy users don't want to spend a lot of time searching for restaurants they already know.

**Possible Solution:**

A **Favorites** feature.

Users could:

- Save favorite restaurants
- Find them quickly
- Reduce browsing time
- Order familiar meals more easily

### Important

At this stage, the solution is still an **idea**.

It should eventually be validated with users.

---

# 21. Example: Favorites Feature

The designer observes that Reena is very busy and doesn't always have time to browse for the perfect meal.

She may have restaurants she orders from frequently.

Therefore:

> A feature that allows users to favorite restaurants and easily access them later could potentially help busy users.

This connects:

**User behavior → User need → Possible feature**

# 22. Empathy Starts Before and Continues After Interviews

Empathy isn't something you do only during an interview.

## Before Interviews

- Understand the target audience
- Perform empathy exercises
- Identify assumptions
- Refine interview questions

## During Interviews

- Listen carefully
- Understand the user's perspective
- Ask follow-up questions
- Avoid judgment
- Follow the user's lead

## After Interviews

- Analyze what users said
- Identify patterns
- Understand needs
- Identify opportunities
- Develop possible solutions

---

# 23. Key Takeaways

### Empathy Exercise

A pre-interview activity where designers imagine possible user experiences to improve their research approach.

### User Profile

A short fictional biography used for informal empathy exercises.

### User Persona

A more formal representation of common characteristics discovered through research.

### Perspective Taking

Trying to understand a situation from the user's point of view.

### Assumptions

Things the designer believes might be true but has not validated with evidence.

### Leading Question

A question that pushes or assumes a particular type of answer.

### Neutral Question

A question that allows the participant to describe their experience without being pushed toward a specific answer.

### Follow-Up Question

A question asked based on something the participant has already mentioned.

### Pattern

A common behavior, need, or experience that appears across multiple users.

### Possible Solution

A feature or design idea that could address an identified user need.
