# 1. Research Goals

## What is a Research Goal?

A research goal defines **what you want to learn** from your users.

It is NOT an interview question.

### Example

**Research Goal:**

> Understand the challenges people experience when ordering food.

The goal gives direction to the entire research process.

## Good Research Goals

Research goals should help you understand:

- User processes
- User behaviors
- User needs
- User frustrations
- User experiences
- User emotions
- Pain points

### Simple Mental Model

> **WHAT do I need to learn?**

---

# 2. Participant Characteristics

After deciding what you want to learn, you need to determine:

> **WHO can give me useful information?**

Participant characteristics describe the types of users you want to include in your research.

## Possible Characteristics

- Age
- Location
- Occupation
- Experience level
- Behaviors
- Frequency of product usage
- Accessibility needs
- Other characteristics relevant to the research

## Important Principle

Choose participants whose experiences are relevant to the problem being researched.

Different types of users may experience the same product very differently.

# 3. Interview Questions

Interview questions are the tools used to **discover information from participants**.

They should connect directly to the research goals.

## Five Characteristics of Good Interview Questions

### 1. Relevant

The question should help answer your research goal.

### 2. Open-ended

The participant should be able to explain their experience instead of simply answering:

> Yes / No

### 3. Clear

Questions should be simple, conversational, and easy to understand.

### 4. Neutral

The question should not push the participant toward a particular answer.

### 5. Designed for Follow-up

The answer should give you opportunities to explore further.

Useful follow-ups include:

- "Tell me more about that."
- "Why?"
- "What happened next?"
- "Can you give me an example?"
- "When did that happen?"
- "How did that make you feel?"

---

# 4. Research Goal vs Interview Question

This distinction is important.

## Research Goal

> What do I want to discover?

## Interview Question

> What can I ask to help me discover it?

### Example

**Research Goal:**

> Understand the challenges people experience when ordering food.

**Interview Question:**

> "Can you walk me through the last time you ordered food?"

The goal defines **what you want to learn**.

The question is the **method for learning it**.

---

# 5. Screener Survey

A screener survey is a set of questions used to determine whether a potential participant fits the requirements of the research study.

Think of it as a **filter**.

### Purpose

Instead of interviewing random people, the screener helps identify people who match your target participant characteristics.

### Example

If your research needs people who order food at least once a week, the screener could determine whether someone meets that requirement.

## Important

> **Screener ≠ Interview**

A screener determines:

> "Does this person fit the study?"

An interview determines:

> "What can I learn from this person?"

# 6. Representative Sample

A representative sample is a smaller group of participants that aims to reflect important characteristics of the larger target population.

## Why It Matters

If you only research one narrow type of user, you may create a distorted understanding of your users.

Different users can have different:

- Needs
- Behaviors
- Experiences
- Problems
- Accessibility requirements
- Perspectives

Therefore, research should try to include different relevant user groups.

## Important Principle

> **Don't let your participant group create a misleading picture of your users.**

Perfect representation is not always possible because research has limitations such as:

- Time
- Money
- Resources
- Access to participants

# 7. Recruiting Participants

Once you know:

- What you want to learn
- Who you need
- Who qualifies

You need to find those people.

This is called **participant recruitment**.

The recruitment method depends on:

- Company
- Product
- Budget
- Time
- Target audience
- Accessibility of users

---

# 8. Recruitment Methods

## 1. Personal Network

Recruit people you already know.

Examples:

- Friends
- Family
- Colleagues

Useful when you're learning UX and have limited resources.

---

## 2. Existing User Base

If a company already has users, researchers can recruit participants from that existing user base.

Common in real-world UX research.

---

## 3. Online Recruitment

Participants can be found through:

- Social media
- Online communities
- Research participant platforms

Important:

> Follow the rules of online communities before recruiting participants.

---

## 4. Hallway Testing

Hallway testing means approaching people in person and asking them to participate.

The idea is to find people in places where the target audience is likely to be present.

### Advantages

- Fast
- Low cost
- Useful for small studies

### Limitation

The people you find may not accurately represent your target users.

---

## 5. Third-Party Recruiting Agencies

Companies can hire research recruiting agencies to find participants.

### Advantages

- Saves researcher time
- Can reach diverse participants
- Useful when finding specific users is difficult

### Disadvantage

- Requires budget

# 9. Overall UX Research Flow

The concepts fit together like this:

**Research Goal**
>
What do I need to learn?

↓

**Target Users**
>
Who experiences this problem?

↓

**Participant Characteristics**
>
What types of users do I need?

↓

**Screener**
>
Does this person fit the study?

↓

**Recruitment**
>
Where/how do I find them?

↓

**Interview**
>
How can I learn about their experiences?

↓

**Research Findings**
>
What patterns, needs, behaviors, and pain points did I discover?

---

# Core Mental Model

Remember the overall logic rather than memorizing the course text:

**WHAT → WHO → FILTER → FIND → LEARN**

### WHAT
What do I need to learn?
→ Research goals

### WHO
Who can provide useful information?
→ Target users / participant characteristics

### FILTER
Who actually fits the research?
→ Screener

### FIND
How do I find those people?
→ Recruitment

### LEARN
How do I understand their experiences?
→ Interviews

---

# Key Takeaways

- Research goals define **what you want to learn**.
- Participant characteristics define **who you need to learn from**.
- Interview questions define **how you gather information**.
- A screener helps identify **qualified participants**.
- A representative sample tries to reflect the diversity of the target users.
- Recruitment is the process of **finding participants**.
- Different recruitment methods have different costs, benefits, and limitations.
- Good UX research focuses on **understanding users**, not proving that your solution is correct.

## One-Line Summary

> **UX research starts by deciding what you need to understand, identifying who can provide that understanding, finding the right participants, and learning from their real experiences.**
