# 1. What is User Research?

**User Research = studying real users to understand their needs, problems, behavior, and experiences.**

The goal is not to prove that our idea is correct.

The goal is to **discover what is actually happening in the user's life.**

### Small Example

**Assumption:**

> “Workers need Bluetooth in their hard hats.”

Instead of immediately building Bluetooth, we talk to workers and understand their actual work experience.

**Possible user response:**

> “I don't really use my phone while working. My bigger problem is that the helmet becomes uncomfortable after a few hours.”

### What we learn

The user's real problem may be **different from our assumption**.

---

# 2. Why User Research is Important

Research helps us make better decisions and avoid wasting time and money on things users don't actually need.

### Without Research

**Assumption → Build → User doesn't need it → Time/Money wasted**

### With Research

**Research → Understand Problem → Find Insight → Make Decision**

### Small Example

Company thinks:

> “Bluetooth will make the helmet more useful.”

Research shows:

> “Workers need to hear coworkers and warning sounds, so they rarely use phones while working.”

### What we learn

The original idea may not solve an important problem.

---

# 3. Plan the Research

Before talking to users, decide:

- What do we want to learn?
- Who should we talk to?
- Where should we talk to them?
- How will we conduct the research?
- What questions should we ask?

### Small Example

**Objective:**

> Understand the problems construction workers experience while wearing helmets.

**Participants:**

> Construction workers who regularly wear safety helmets.

### What we learn

Good research starts with a **clear objective**, rather than randomly asking questions.

---

# 4. Study People in Their Context

Try to understand users in the environment where they actually perform the activity.

**Context = the situation, environment, people, tools, and conditions surrounding the user's activity.**

### Example

Instead of only asking:

> “Is your helmet comfortable?”

Observe the worker at the construction site.

You notice:

> The worker adjusts the helmet several times.

You ask:

> “I noticed you adjusted your helmet several times. Can you tell me about that?”

**User:**

> “It becomes loose when I move around.”

### What we learn

We discovered a possible **fit/stability problem** that the user didn't mention initially.

---

# 5. Ask Open-Ended Questions

Avoid questions that push users toward a particular answer.

### ❌ Leading Question

> “Would Bluetooth help you?”

**User:**

> “Yeah, probably.”

We learned very little.

### ✅ Open-Ended Question

> “How do you currently handle phone calls while working?”

**User:**

> “If it's important, I remove my gloves and answer. Otherwise, I ignore it.”

### What we learn

We understand the user's **current behavior** instead of simply getting a yes/no answer.

---

# 6. Understand Behavior and Meaning

Don't only ask:

> **What do users do?**

Also understand:

> **Why do they do it?**

### Example

**Observation:**

Worker removes the helmet when leaving the worksite.

**Researcher:**

> “Why do you remove it?”

**User:**

> “I feel uncomfortable when people look at me wearing it outside.”

### What we learn

The problem isn't necessarily helmet functionality.

There may be a **social/emotional aspect** to the experience.

### Remember

**Behavior → Why → Meaning → Insight**

---

# 7. Ask Follow-Up / Probing Questions

The first answer is often only the beginning.

### Example

**Researcher:**

> “What problems do you have with the helmet?”

**User:**

> “It's uncomfortable.”

Don't immediately move to the next question.

Ask:

> “What makes it uncomfortable?”

**User:**

> “It gets really hot.”

Ask:

> “When do you notice that most?”

**User:**

> “After working for three or four hours.”

### What we learn

We moved from:

> “It's uncomfortable.”

to:

> **“Heat becomes a problem during long periods of use.”**

That's much more useful.

---

# 8. Listen for Unexpected Problems

Don't only listen for answers related to the questions you prepared.

Users may reveal something completely unexpected.

### Example

**Researcher:**

> “Tell me about your normal workday.”

**User:**

> “The work is fine, but I hate going outside for lunch wearing all this safety equipment.”

This wasn't what the researcher expected.

The researcher follows up:

> “What makes that uncomfortable?”

**User:**

> “People stare at me. I feel awkward.”

### What we learn

The research may uncover a **completely different problem** from the one the company originally planned to solve.

This is one of the most important purposes of user research.

---

# 9. Analyze and Organize Information

After interviews, you'll have lots of different answers.

Organize them and look for relationships.

### Example

**Worker 1:**

> “My head gets sweaty.”

**Worker 2:**

> “The helmet gets hot.”

**Worker 3:**

> “I remove it during breaks.”

**Worker 4:**

> “It's uncomfortable after a few hours.”

### What we learn

Instead of treating these as four unrelated comments, we can group them under:

> **Comfort / Heat**

This is the beginning of analysis.

---

# 10. Find Patterns and Generate Insights

When similar things appear across multiple users, we may have a **pattern**.

### Example

4 out of 5 workers mention discomfort after several hours.

### Pattern

> Long-term comfort is repeatedly mentioned.

### Possible Insight

> Workers may need better long-term comfort from their safety equipment.

### Remember

**Data = What users said/did**

**Pattern = Something repeated**

**Insight = What that pattern might mean**

---

# 11. Turn Insights into Decisions

Research should ultimately help people make decisions.

### Example

**Research finding:**

> Multiple workers experience heat and discomfort after several hours.

### Possible decision

The team investigates:

- Better ventilation
- Lighter materials
- Improved padding
- Better fit

Research doesn't automatically give the final design.

It gives the team **evidence to decide what to investigate or change next.**

---

# 12. OTHER User Research Methods includes

**User research is the bigger field.**

Interviews are just **one research method**.

- **Interview** → Talk directly with users to understand their experiences and problems.
- **Observation** → Watch users while they actually perform a task.
- **Survey** → Ask the same questions to many users, usually through a Google Form.
- **Usability Testing** → Give users a product/prototype and see how easily they can use it.
- **Field Study** → Study users in their real-world environment.
- **Diary Study** → Ask users to record their experiences over a period of time.

### Example

A worker says:

> “The helmet is comfortable.”

But you observe them repeatedly adjusting it.

That difference between **what people say and what they do** can be worth investigating.

---

# 13. Research Can Help Decide What NOT to Build

Research doesn't always result in:

> “Great! Let's build this.”

Sometimes the most valuable decision is:

> **“Don't build it.”**

### Hard-Hat Example

Company planned:

> Bluetooth + advanced features

Research discovered:

> Workers had other concerns and rarely needed the proposed functionality.

The company decided not to continue with the original product.

### Key Lesson

**Stopping a risky product idea can also be a successful research outcome.**

# The 5-Step User Research Process

The entire process can be remembered in **5 major steps**:

## 1. Plan

Decide:

- What do we want to learn?
- Who will we study?
- How will we conduct the research?

↓

## 2. Study People in Context

Go where users actually work/live/use the product.

Observe their real environment and activities.

↓

## 3. Understand Behavior and Meaning

Don't just ask:

> “What do they do?”

Also ask:

> “Why do they do it?”

Understand the meaning behind their behavior.

↓

## 4. Analyze the Information

Organize what you collected.

Look for:

- Patterns
- Repeated problems
- Differences
- Important observations
- Unexpected findings

↓

## 5. Turn Insights into Decisions

Use the research findings to guide:

- Design
- Product
- Service
- Business decisions
- Or the decision **not to build something**

---

# Simple Formula to Remember

**PLAN**

↓  

**STUDY PEOPLE IN CONTEXT**

↓  

**UNDERSTAND BEHAVIOR + MEANING**

↓  

**ANALYZE**

↓  

**INSIGHTS → DECISIONS**

---

## One Big Takeaway

> **Don't enter research with the mindset: “I already know the problem and I just need users to confirm it.”**

Instead:

> **“I have a question. Let me understand the user's world and discover what is actually happening.”**
