# 1. Screener Survey

## What is a Screener Survey?

A **screener survey** is a set of questions used to determine whether potential participants meet the requirements of a UX research study.

Think of it as a **filter**.

### Example

You need people who:

- Order food at least once a week
- Live in a city or suburb
- Are within a specific age range

You might receive 100 people interested in participating.

The screener helps identify which people actually fit the study.

```text
Potential Participants
        ↓
     Screener
        ↓
Qualified Participants
        ↓
   Select Participants
        ↓
     Interview
```

### Core Idea

> **A screener answers: "Does this person fit my research?"**

---

# 2. Demographics in Screening

A screener can collect demographic information when it is relevant to the research.

Examples include:

- Age
- Geographic location
- Job title
- Industry
- Gender

However, demographic questions can be sensitive.

## Good Practice

When collecting demographic information:

- Ask only what is relevant
- Be respectful and inclusive
- Explain why the information is being collected when appropriate
- Make sensitive demographic questions optional when possible

### Core Principle

> **Don't collect personal information unless there is a research reason for needing it.**

# 3. Inclusive Research

UX research should consider people with different:

- Backgrounds
- Perspectives
- Abilities
- Experiences

This helps designers avoid creating products that work well only for a narrow group of users.

### Why It Matters

Different users may experience the same product differently.

For example, an interaction that seems obvious to one user might be difficult for someone with an accessibility need.

### Core Principle

> **Design research should help uncover the experiences of the people who will actually use the product.**

---

# 4. Representative Sample

A **representative sample** is a smaller group of participants that aims to reflect important characteristics of the larger target population.

You usually cannot interview every potential user.

Instead, you select a manageable group that represents relevant types of users.

### Why It Matters

If you interview only one narrow group, your research may give you a distorted understanding of the overall user population.

### Important

A perfectly representative sample is not always possible because of:

- Time
- Budget
- Resources
- Access to participants

The important thing is to **recognize the risk of an unrepresentative sample**.

# 5. Participant Recruitment

Once you know:

- What you want to learn
- Who your target users are
- What characteristics participants should have
- Who qualifies through the screener

You need to find those people.

This process is called **participant recruitment**.

### Core Idea

> **Recruitment is the process of finding people who can participate in your research.**

---

# 6. Recruitment Methods

Different projects require different recruitment methods.

The choice can depend on:

- Company
- Product
- Budget
- Time constraints
- Target audience
- Accessibility of users

---

## 6.1 Personal Network

Recruit people you already know.

Examples:

- Friends
- Family
- Colleagues

### Advantage

- Easy
- Fast
- Usually free

### Limitation

Your personal network may not accurately represent your target users.

---

## 6.2 Existing User Base

If a company already has users, researchers can recruit participants from that existing user base.

### Advantage

Participants are already connected to the product or organization.

### Common Use

This is common in real-world UX research.

---

## 6.3 Online Recruitment

Participants can be recruited through:

- Social media
- Online communities
- Research participant platforms

### Advantage

You can reach people outside your personal network.

### Limitation

You still need to screen participants to make sure they fit the research requirements.

### Important

When recruiting through an online community:

> Check the community rules or ask an administrator for permission before recruiting.

---

## 6.4 Hallway Testing

**Hallway testing** means approaching people in person and asking them to participate in research.

The idea is to find people in locations where your target users are likely to be.

### Example

If researching a product for dog owners, a dog park may be a useful location to find potential participants.

### Advantages

- Fast
- Low cost
- Useful for small studies
- Can be done with limited resources

### Limitation

The people you find may not actually represent your target users.

---

## 6.5 Third-Party Recruiting Agencies

Organizations can hire specialized recruiting agencies to find research participants.

### Advantages

- Saves researcher time
- Can find specific types of participants
- Can help reach diverse users

### Limitation

- Requires additional budget

---

# 7. Screener vs Recruitment

These two concepts are related but different.

## Recruitment

> **Where/how do I find participants?**

Examples:

- Social media
- Friends
- Existing customers
- Online communities
- Recruiting agencies

## Screener

> **Does this person qualify for my study?**

### Example

```text
Recruitment
     ↓
100 people respond
     ↓
Screener
     ↓
30 people qualify
     ↓
Select participants
     ↓
Interview
```

# 8. Overall Research Recruitment Flow

The complete process can be understood as:

```text
Research Goals
      ↓
What do I need to learn?
      ↓
Target Users
      ↓
Who experiences this problem?
      ↓
Participant Characteristics
      ↓
What types of users do I need?
      ↓
Screener Survey
      ↓
Who actually qualifies?
      ↓
Recruitment
      ↓
Where/how do I find them?
      ↓
Participant Selection
      ↓
Who should participate?
      ↓
Interview
      ↓
Learn from their experiences
```

---

# 9. Key Takeaways

- A **screener survey** filters potential participants based on research requirements.
- Demographic information should be collected thoughtfully and only when relevant.
- UX research should consider diverse backgrounds, perspectives, and abilities.
- A **representative sample** aims to reflect important characteristics of the larger target population.
- **Recruitment** is the process of finding potential research participants.
- Different recruitment methods have different advantages and limitations.
- Personal networks are convenient but may not represent the target population.
- Existing users can be valuable participants in real-world research.
- Online recruitment can provide access to a wider range of people.
- Hallway testing is fast and inexpensive but can produce an unrepresentative sample.
- Third-party recruiting agencies save time but require budget.
- **Recruitment finds people; screening determines whether they qualify.**

# Core Mental Model

Remember:

> **WHAT → WHO → FILTER → FIND → SELECT → LEARN**

### WHAT
What do I need to learn?
→ **Research Goals**

### WHO
Who experiences this problem?
→ **Target Users**

### FILTER
Who qualifies?
→ **Screener**

### FIND
Where/how do I find them?
→ **Recruitment**

### SELECT
Who should participate?
→ **Participant Selection / Representative Sample**

### LEARN
What can I discover from them?
→ **Interview**

---

# One-Line Summary

> **UX participant recruitment is about finding the right people, filtering them against your research requirements, and selecting a group that can provide useful and diverse insights about your target users.**
